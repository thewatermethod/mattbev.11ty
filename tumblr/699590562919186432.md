> > > > > > > > > China Miéville: Marxism and Halloween - Socialism 2013
> > > > > > > > > 
> > > > > > > > > A bit of Halloween-related theory. Fascinating speech about how we ought to embrace Halloween as leftists. Generally pretty light-hearted and funny, but there’s a lot of really captivating stuff in here. I had never heard of Gothic Marxism before watching this video, and it sounds like something I really need to explore. As someone with a deep respect for the sense of awe that lingers across human culture, and as someone who would never support a socialist revolution if it meant the eradication of passion and fun and spiritual reverence and awe (it most certainly won’t), I highly recommend this one.
> > > > > > > > 
> > > > > > > > 🎃👻🚩🏴
> > > > > > > 
> > > > > > > Some of the points he makes are really well illustrated in a book called “Hogfather” by Terry Pratchett…
> > > > > > > 
> > > > > > > Also: society cycles in what it’s afraid of. Sometimes it’s vampires again (the old powerful aristocracies), sometimes it’s extraterrestrials(strangers and other cultures), and right now it seems to be zombies. The bourgeoisie is afraid of the nameless hordes coming for their blood, something even their wealth won’t save them from. And “normal” people who get “infected”? They join the zombies in their single-minded drive to eat what’s left of “Western civilisation” or the “American Way of Life”.
> > > > > > > 
> > > > > > > Well, I think they aren’t wrong, and it’s really fascinating that zombie movies tend to crop up at times of big social unrest, mass protests and clamouring for justice and putting an end to those who kill us.
> > > > > > > 
> > > > > > > There is a reason I’m so very fond of the slogan “Eat the rich.”
> > > > > > > 
> > > > > > > Source:
> > > > > > 
> > > > > > Reminds me somewhat of the book “Splatter Capital” by Mark Stevens, which argues that the resurgences of splatter cinema happen pretty much in sequence with major capitalist crises, as there’s a subconscious recognition of the gory, violent, visceral underpinnings of capitalist society surging in the cultural consciousness.
> > > > > 
> > > > > I like my politics like I like my holidays: spooky
> > > > 
> > > > I like a lot of the ideas here, but I’m not used to the complicated academic way of talking, so I made my own translation of the talking points.
> > > > 
> > > > 1.  Halloween, as a nighttime holiday, is a break from work and the usual 9-to-5 calendar. So Halloween is socialist in that way.
> > > > 2.  Halloween can be a celebration of marginalized people: during Halloween, we celebrate and ally ourselves with monsters. But powerful people in society make monsters and outcasts out of marginalized people.
> > > > 3.  Some people claim that our money-crazy culture makes Halloween about buying things and sexist costumes and trying to selfishly hunt down as much candy as possible, but it doesn’t have to be about that.
> > > > 4.  The supernatural aspects of Halloween don’t have to mean that we’re celebrating being delusional. They can be about honoring the outcasts of society, the weird parts of our culture that aren’t directed toward making profit, and the rich sources of story and culture in fiction.
> > > > 5.  Being spooky is about being human, because it requires imagining/dreading the future in a way that only humans do.
> > > > 6.  In modern times, there’s a special kind of being spooked, which is when you don’t believe in the supernatural but it scares you anyways. That means that this Halloween stuff is for “modern rational people” too.
> > > > 7.  Because Halloween should be about celebrating the genuinely spooky part of human nature, Halloween should be about actually scary stuff, not branded mascots.
> > > 
> > > Lots of people dress up as things they admire and want to be like, like their favourite characters, or some profession they think is cool. It’s not just about what we fear, but what we desire.

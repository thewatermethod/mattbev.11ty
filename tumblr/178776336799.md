> Not Quite the Last Man on Earth by Nathaniel Thompson
> -----------------------------------------------------
> 
> The fantasy of being the last human being on the planet is one that seems to have been around forever, but it’s actually something that has only been around in pop culture for roughly six decades. Going hand in hand with that idea is the global outbreak, something stemming from the plagues that swept medieval Europe, now known as pandemics. You can explore films tackling the idea of diseases running rampant and wreaking havoc on FilmStruck, though few have been as apocalyptic or as weirdly memorable as THE OMEGA MAN (’71).
> 
> ![](https://thewatermethod.com/tumblr/thewatermethod.tumblr.com/media/fcb109c59584d10e569f457a2a60789f9242cb2fd101f4e10bc4aaed5f16c8f1.jpg)
> 
> The second of three official screen adaptations of Richard Matheson’s _I Am Legend_, THE OMEGA MAN is sandwiched in between a take with Vincent Price, THE LAST MAN ON EARTH (‘64), and the very long-gestating I AM LEGEND (2007) with Will Smith. Matheson’s novel is perhaps the Rosetta Stone of both modern end-of-the-world and zombie scenarios, fusing the two together with monsters, technically called vampires, but far closer to what George A. Romero and his successors would unleash on movie screens.
> 
> Although it’s an avowed lifetime favorite film of Tim Burton (among others), THE OMEGA MAN isn’t quite a vampire or zombie film. Instead it’s a decidedly post-Charles Manson parable with its sole living human, Neville (Charlton Heston), tormented by and constantly fighting off albino, white-eyed assailants in cult-style robes. It’s also the first adaptation that has a little fun with the concept, having Heston cruise around the deserted streets of Los Angeles by day and repeatedly watching the one film print he can run, WOODSTOCK (‘70). It’s a lonely but oddly compelling hook that captured the imaginations of moviegoers at the time and still works like a charm today; even being echoed in the later version by having Will Smith recite every single word to SHREK (’01) by heart.
> 
> ![](https://thewatermethod.com/tumblr/thewatermethod.tumblr.com/media/461a1d72331e0c5a55bbfc908a52b6df4bdab3bb89e53039d9cf145ebbd01c1a.jpg)
> 
> More importantly though, THE OMEGA MAN has had longstanding ripples in subsequent films and television shows to this day. The obvious series touchstone is the recently ended _The Last Man on Earth_, of course, with its comic spin on the idea, but others include NIGHT OF THE COMET (‘84), 28 DAYS LATER (2002) and its 2007 sequel, 28 WEEKS LATER. This being the ‘70s, you also get a healthy dollop of Christian symbolism with an ending that plays straight to the Jesus movement that had been stirring in SoCal since the late ‘60s. The strange clash that resulted in that movement—the counterculture merging with evangelical Christianity—is a perfect mirror for this film, with its uneasy stance as a shell-shocked commentary on the recent turbulence involving issues of race, sexuality and most notably, the generation gap.
> 
> ![](https://thewatermethod.com/tumblr/thewatermethod.tumblr.com/media/af477925bea81d2b9735c1eb028f5359d57e5a2da33898e4c38c1be1af94ce40.jpg)
> 
> Heston, a prototypical Hollywood white male savior figure, if there ever was one, still gets to serve his role here but feels like an odd man out right from the beginning. He has no support network, and everyone around him is so physically different they might as well be from a different planet. That changes slightly with the arrival of Lisa, played by the underrated (and now much-missed) Rosalind Cash whose interracial romance with Heston was a major talking point at the time. The casting of Cash (whose race wasn’t a factor in early drafts) is fascinating and gives the film a particular potency, given that the worst fate one can suffer here is the loss of pigmentation that reflects what amounts to a degrading or entire loss of the soul and self-control. Some critics chided what they perceived as pandering to the Black Power movement (something that was also leveled at the equally fascinating and unsettling LIVE AND LET DIE two years later), but in the long run it proved to be the right call as the film uses race, age and gender to demonstrate how these differences are not only what make us human but could help us weather even the darkest and deadliest of storms.

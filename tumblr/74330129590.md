I generally don’t read much manga, but lately [a friend](http://perspicacity1.tumblr.com) and I have been exchanging books weekly in order to save money and broaden horizons. I have been giving him the books that I enjoyed most when I was younger, the British Vertigo Invasion writers, Claremont/Byrne X-Men, and Busiek’s Conan stuff for example.

He’s been giving me manga. Results are mixed. I loved [Abandon the Old in Tokyo](http://www.amazon.com/Abandon-Old-Tokyo-Yoshihiro-Tatsumi/dp/1770460772/ref=sr_1_1?ie=UTF8&qid=1390523262&sr=8-1&keywords=abandon+the+old). Most everything else, well, I find reading from right to left distracting, and translation issues annoying.

One of the things that manga does offer over Western comics is a far greater diversity of topics. Oishinbo, for example, is apparently a long-running comic about cooking of all things. What is the American counterpart to that?

Next to the aforementioned Abandon the Old in Tokyo, this is by far my favorite manga that I’ve read. I found the subject matter enlightening and interesting enough. Apparently for English translation, the material was reordered so as to be grouped by cuisine instead of chronological order. 

Not having read more, I can’t say how appropriate or not that is, but this book, which is a series of vignettes on traditional Japanese cuisine, flows nicely. The framing story, about a young foodie estranged from his domineering food obsessed father, is interesting and kept to a (relatively) low level of melodrama. The art, likewise, is well-done. It is easy to differentiate the characters, the “acting” is not over the top, and the food is lovingly rendered.

One problem with stories like this is that they tend to wander into simply dumping information onto your plate en masse. Oishinbo does occasionally blast you with lectures on sashimi or whatever. But! Even though passages like that do sometimes become difficult to read, I must admit they do actually make sense in the context of the narrative.

I’m certainly glad a book like this exists; it speaks to the power and diversity of the format as a teaching tool and is the kind of thing you can recommend to basically anyone. Everyone, after all, has to eat.
